# Digital Restoration of Motion Blur and Atmospheric Turbulence

This repository investigates classical image restoration techniques for recovering images degraded by **motion blur**, **atmospheric turbulence**, and **additive Gaussian noise**. The project focuses on frequency-domain degradation modeling and deconvolution-based restoration, with particular attention to the stability, robustness, and reconstruction quality of different filtering approaches.

---

## Overview

Image restoration is a fundamental inverse problem in digital image processing. During image acquisition or transmission, physical phenomena such as camera motion, optical distortions, atmospheric effects, and sensor noise can significantly degrade visual information. Recovering the original image from these observations is challenging because the degradation process is often ill-conditioned and highly sensitive to noise.

This project provides:

- Realistic simulation of image degradation processes.
- Implementation of classical restoration algorithms.
- Comparative analysis of reconstruction quality under different degradation scenarios.
- Quantitative evaluation using standard image-quality metrics.

---

## Objectives

### Degradation Modeling

The project simulates three common sources of image degradation:

- **Linear Motion Blur** generated by uniform relative motion between the camera and the scene.
- **Atmospheric Turbulence Blur** modeled through Kolmogorov-based isotropic degradation.
- **Additive Gaussian Noise** with controllable variance.

### Restoration Methods

Three frequency-domain restoration techniques are implemented and compared:

- **Inverse Filtering**
- **Wiener Filtering**
- **Constrained Least Squares Filtering (CLSF)**

### Performance Evaluation

Restoration quality is assessed using:

- **Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR)**
- **Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)**

## Theoretical Background

Image degradation is modeled in the frequency domain as:

$$
G(u,v) = H(u,v)F(u,v) + N(u,v)
$$

where:

- $F(u,v)$ is the original image,
- $H(u,v)$ is the degradation function (Optical Transfer Function),
- $N(u,v)$ represents additive noise,
- $G(u,v)$ is the observed degraded image.

Restoration aims to estimate $F(u,v$ from the observed data $G(u,v)$.

---

## Degradation Models

### Linear Motion Blur

Uniform linear motion is modeled through a sinc-based transfer function:

$$
H(u,v) =
T \cdot \mathrm{sinc}(ua + vb)
e^{-j\pi(ua+vb)}
$$

where:

- $a$ and $b$ describe the motion direction,
- $T$ is the exposure time.

This model introduces periodic spectral zeros, making the restoration problem particularly challenging.

### Atmospheric Turbulence

Atmospheric turbulence is represented using a Kolmogorov-inspired isotropic low-pass filter:

$$
H(u,v) =
e^{-k(u^2 + v^2)^{5/6}}
$$

where $k$ controls the turbulence intensity.

Unlike motion blur, this degradation progressively attenuates high-frequency components without introducing exact spectral zeros.

### Additive Gaussian Noise

Noise is modeled as:

$$
\eta(x,y) \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)
$$

where $sigma^2$ denotes the noise variance.

---

## Restoration Algorithms

### Inverse Filter

The inverse filter directly inverts the degradation model:

$$
\hat{F}(u,v) =
\frac{G(u,v)}{H(u,v)}
$$

Although theoretically capable of perfect reconstruction, it is extremely sensitive to noise. Whenever $H(u,v)$ approaches zero, noise amplification becomes severe, often dominating the reconstructed image. A thresholding mechanism is therefore introduced to avoid numerical instabilities.

---

### Wiener Filter

The Wiener filter addresses the instability of direct inversion by minimizing the mean squared reconstruction error:

$$
H_W(u,v)=
\frac{H^*(u,v)}
{|H(u,v)|^2 + K}
$$

where:

- $H^*(u,v)$ is the complex conjugate of $H(u,v)$,
- $K$ approximates the inverse signal-to-noise ratio.

The parameter $K$ is estimated dynamically from image and noise statistics, providing a practical balance between deblurring and noise suppression.

---

### Constrained Least Squares Filter (CLSF)

CLSF formulates image restoration as a regularized optimization problem:

$$
\hat{F}(u,v)=
\left[
\frac{H^*(u,v)}
{|H(u,v)|^2 + \gamma |P(u,v)|^2}
\right]
G(u,v)
$$

where:

- $P(u,v)$ is the Fourier transform of the discrete Laplacian operator,
- $\gamma$ is the regularization parameter.

The Laplacian penalty suppresses noise amplification while preserving important structural information such as edges and contours. The value of \(\gamma\) is estimated automatically through an iterative bisection procedure that matches the reconstruction residual to the expected noise power.

## Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on standard grayscale images from the `scikit-image` dataset, including:

- Astronaut
- Coffee
- Cat

Five degradation configurations were evaluated, combining different levels of blur severity and Gaussian noise variance.

For each configuration:

1. The original image is degraded.
2. All restoration algorithms are applied.
3. Reconstruction quality is measured using PSNR and SSIM.
4. Visual and numerical comparisons are performed.

---

## Results Summary

### Inverse Filter

- Performs well only in nearly noise-free conditions.
- Highly unstable in the presence of noise.
- Motion blur degradation is particularly problematic because spectral zeros cause severe amplification artifacts.

### Wiener Filter

- Produces stable and reliable reconstructions.
- Effectively suppresses noise.
- Tends to introduce mild smoothing of fine image details.

### Constrained Least Squares Filter

- Consistently delivers the best visual quality.
- Preserves edges and structural information more effectively.
- Achieves the highest SSIM scores in most noisy scenarios.

|------------------|----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Astronaut** (Motion Blur + Noise) | SSIM | 0.4412 | 0.0150 | 0.3830 | **0.6141** |
| | PSNR | 18.20 | 5.73 | 20.38 | **22.07** |
| **Astronaut** (Pure Blur, No Noise) | SSIM | 0.5210 | 0.7752 | **0.7761** | 0.7752 |
| | PSNR | 19.45 | **26.77** | 26.74 | **26.77** |

| | PSNR | 14.10 | 4.68 | 19.39 | **20.61** |

### Main Findings

- The inverse filter is only suitable when noise is negligible.
- Wiener filtering offers the best trade-off between stability and reconstruction accuracy.
- CLSF achieves the strongest structural preservation and generally provides the highest perceptual quality.
- Under noisy conditions, CLSF frequently outperforms Wiener filtering in terms of SSIM while maintaining competitive PSNR values.
